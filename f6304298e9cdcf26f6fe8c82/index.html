Mon 19 Oct · The system beats the training
credit: Sean Wangpending
Check before approving
  • none. (Sean Wang's exact Medium article URL wasn't captured, so the link is his site homepage. Swap in the article link if Ian has it.)
  • Confirm the supervisor story is true for you.

Post

Your transformation didn't fail in the training room. It failed the Monday after. Sean Wang puts it perfectly: "The operating system is stronger than the training." I've seen it again and again. A supervisor comes back from problem-solving training, fired up. His boss says: "We don't have time for that. Just get it running." Lesson learned. Not the one we taught. Here's why the system wins: 1. Leaders ask "Who's responsible?" instead of "What happened?" 2. Firefighting heroes get rewarded. Prevention is invisible. 3. Three approvals for any decision, so problems die in the handoff. 4. The visual board nobody senior looks at becomes wallpaper. 5. New work gets added and nothing gets taken away. When people say "I just don't have time", that isn't resistance. It's an accurate description of the system. You can't train your way out of a system problem. You have to change what leaders ask about, reward and measure. Is your organisation changing the people, or the system around them? Stop sending people on courses. Start fixing what they come back to.

Tue 20 Oct · AI is hiding your team's burnout
credit: Illia Smoliienko, Fast Companypending
Check before approving

Post

Your team is hitting every target. That might be the warning sign. Illia Smoliienko wrote something in Fast Company this week that stopped me. For 20 years his early-warning sign of overload was simple: someone's work slowed down. AI has broken that signal. People keep hitting their numbers right up to the point of burnout. Here's why it's happening: • Time saved by AI is quietly spent on more work, not on recovery. • Reviewing and fixing AI output is real work that no metric counts. • The bar for "a normal day's output" rises without anyone saying so. In my coaching, the teams I worry about most are never the slow ones now. They're the ones who look fine on the dashboard. What I tell leaders: 1. Decide in advance what saved time is for. 2. Make the invisible review work visible. 3. Ask what a result cost the team, not just whether it landed. Velocity isn't wellbeing. When did you last ask your team how they're doing, rather than what they've delivered? Stop reading output as health. Start asking what it cost.

Wed 21 Oct · Outcomes over output is too easy
credit: Marty Caganpending
Check before approving
  • none. (Only Cagan's post was captured, not his full article. Ian should read the SVPG article before posting so the framing isn't misrepresented.)
  • Confirm the Gantt-chart planning sessions line is true for you.

Post

"Outcomes over output" is true. That's the problem. Marty Cagan, who has argued for it for 20+ years, now says it's so obvious it misses the hard choice teams actually face. His better framing: predictability versus outcomes. I love this, because nobody in a steering group is against outcomes. What they're really asking for is certainty. Here's why predictability keeps winning: 1. A date on a roadmap feels safer than a bet on a result. 2. Executives get asked "will it land on time?" far more than "did it work?" 3. AI makes building faster, so the question of WHAT to build gets more exposed, not less. I've sat in many quarterly planning sessions where the team promised outcomes and the room asked for a Gantt chart. The honest conversation is this: what predictability are we willing to give up to learn faster? Name the trade-off out loud and the debate gets grown-up very quickly. Does your leadership team want outcomes, or does it want to be told nothing will go wrong? Stop selling outcomes as free. Start negotiating the trade-off.

credit: Sean Connolly, Evinovapending

Post

Adding AI to your current process is the most expensive way to stay the same. Sean Connolly (Evinova's CPTO) said it simply at a recent session: you don't transform a system by optimising its parts. You redesign it. Most AI programmes I see do the opposite. Same handoffs. Same approvals. Same queues. Just with a chatbot on top. Here's why that disappoints: • Speeding up one step just moves the queue to the next one. • The real cost lives in waiting, not working. • Friction in the flow beats features every time. When I map the value stream with a team, we usually find work spends [placeholder]% of its time waiting. AI doesn't touch that. Redesign does. Before you ask "where can we use AI?", ask "if we built this from scratch today, how would the work flow?" The answer is usually uncomfortable. And that's where the value is. Are you redesigning your system, or decorating it? Stop automating the old process. Start redesigning the flow.

Fri 23 Oct · The stories change leaders carry
credit: Honey Dacanaypending
Check before approving
  • none. Ian should confirm that "I've lived at least three of those" is true for him.

Post

The most important moment in your change programme will feel like just another Tuesday. Honey Dacanay has been interviewing change leaders and sponsors across countries and sectors. Her insight: they don't experience change as a list of problems. They experience it as a story, and its meaning often only becomes clear after they've left. She describes four arcs: 1. Success, including knowing when to leave on your own terms. 2. Staying, and slowly being absorbed by the institution. 3. Discovering the organisation's commitment had limits. 4. Leaving too early, then watching them make the same change years later without you. I've lived at least three of those. The fourth one stings. The turning points look obvious in hindsight. In the moment they're a throwaway comment in a steering group, or a sponsor who stops turning up. And when change leaders leave, the institutional knowledge leaves with them. So write it down. Tell the story while you're still in it. Which arc are you in right now, and would you know if it was changing? Stop treating change as a project plan. Start treating it as a story you're part of.

Post

The smartest person in the room is rarely the one who moves things forward. I pay for two AI assistants. One is brilliant. When I need to think something through or get a piece of writing to sing, it's superb. The other is useful. It follows through. That's no slight on the first one. But it reminded me of every leadership team I've worked with. Brilliant and useful aren't the same thing. In tools or in people. Here's what useful looks like: 1. It finishes. The job isn't done when the idea is clear. It's done when it's delivered. 2. It spots the next step without being asked. 3. It doesn't drop things between meetings. 4. It turns up on a dull Tuesday, not just for the big presentation. I've watched brilliant coaches and consultants get cut when budgets tighten, while less dazzling ones get renewed. The difference was rarely talent. It was follow-through. If you're hiring, promoting or building a team, are you rewarding brilliance, or the people who get it done? Stop promoting the cleverest voice. Start rewarding follow-through.

Tue 27 Oct · The question behind the question

Post

The best answer is often to the question nobody asked. I asked my AI assistant to find an email from three years ago. A good search tool would have stopped there. It found the email. Then it looked up the sender, told me what they'd been doing since, and drafted a note to reconnect. I asked for a search. I got a reconnection. That's the difference between answering a question and understanding why someone asked it. It's also the skill I most want to see in leaders. When someone in your team asks you for something: 1. Answer the question they asked. 2. Ask yourself what they're really trying to achieve. 3. Offer the next step, not just the answer. 4. Check you've read it right before you run with it. Step 4 matters. Second-guessing people without checking isn't insight. It's presumption. In coaching execs, the first question is almost never the real one. "How do I fix this team?" often means "How do I stop it all resting on me?" When did someone last answer the question behind your question? Stop answering only what's asked. Start asking what it's for.

Wed 28 Oct · "Done" is the most dangerous word
credit: Ian's own (Substack: Brilliant vs useful)pending
Check before approving
  • Ian to confirm: "I've sat in steering groups where every workstream reported green and nothing had reached a single customer."
  • Placeholder: [SUBSTACK URL: Brilliant vs useful]. The son's job-search story comes from your Substack piece.

Post

"Done" is the most dangerous word in your organisation. My son is looking for work. I asked my AI assistant to find job adverts that might suit him. It did. Then I asked if they could be sent on to him, and that was simply the next step. No starting again. No copying and pasting between windows. It treated my ask as one step in a bigger job. Most organisations do the opposite. Each team finishes its step and throws it over the wall: • Design is "done" when the spec is signed off. • Build is "done" when the ticket is closed. • Ops is "done" when it's handed over. Every status is green. The customer is still waiting. I've sat in steering groups where every workstream reported green and nothing had reached a single customer. The fix isn't more effort. It's three better questions at every step: 1. What is this step for? 2. Who picks it up next? 3. What would make their job easier? Is your team measured on finishing its step, or on the job getting done? Stop celebrating closed tickets. Start following the work to the customer.

Good leaders design work that still runs on a Tuesday when they're not in the building: • Clear triggers, not reminders. • Shared ownership, not a go-to person. • Routines that run on time without anyone being a hero. If you took a fortnight off tomorrow, what would quietly stop? Stop relying on heroes. Start designing work that runs without you.

Your strategy doesn't fail in the boardroom. It fails in the handoff. Two small things changed my working week: • I start something by voice on a dog walk and pick it up in text at my desk. Same conversation, nothing lost. • My reading lands each morning, my notes go into Google Docs, and my phone holds a searchable library with a delete button for what didn't matter. None of that is clever on its own. Together it's a system. Organisations lose far more to broken continuity than to a shortage of brilliant ideas: 1. Context dies between meetings. 2. Decisions get re-made because nobody captured them. 3. Off-site energy drains away the week everyone flies home. 4. New joiners start from zero. What fixes it isn't brilliance. It's process: • One place where the conversation lives. • Decisions written down where people can find them. • A simple weekly rhythm that carries work from one week to the next. Where does context go to die in your organisation? Stop relying on memory. Start building the thread.
